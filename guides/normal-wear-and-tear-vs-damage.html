Normal Wear and Tear vs. Damage: What a Landlord Can Actually Deduct

Landlords can charge for damage. They generally can't charge for the ordinary effects of someone living in a home. The line between the two decides most deposit disputes.

Updated November 18, 2026 · 4 min read · General information, not legal advice

Key takeaways

  • Normal wear and tear is the gradual deterioration that comes from ordinary use. Damage comes from accidents, neglect or misuse.
  • Statutes in California, New York and Texas, among others, say landlords can't deduct for ordinary wear and tear.
  • The age and condition of an item at move-in matter. A 10-year-old carpet doesn't become worthless because you lived on it for a year, but a pet-damaged one is still your responsibility.
  • Photos from move-in and move-out are your best tool for challenging a charge.

Nobody lives in an apartment without leaving a trace. Carpet gets walked on. Paint fades. Blinds yellow. If landlords could bill tenants for all of that, no one would ever see a deposit again, which is exactly why the law draws a line.

The basic rule

Normal wear and tear is the unavoidable deterioration of a home through ordinary, reasonable use over time. Damage is harm beyond that, caused by accident, neglect, abuse or misuse. Landlords may generally deduct reasonable costs for damage, unpaid rent, and cleaning needed to return the unit to its move-in condition. They generally can't deduct for normal wear and tear.

The statutes say this in different words. California allows a landlord to use the deposit to repair damage the tenant caused, but not ordinary wear and tear. Texas says a landlord may not retain any part of a deposit to cover normal wear and tear. New York's law allows deductions for damage beyond normal wear and tear and prohibits deductions for ordinary wear. Most other states follow the same principle.

Wear and tear vs. damage: real examples

AreaUsually normal wear and tearUsually damage
Walls and paintFading, minor scuffs, a few small nail holes from hanging picturesLarge holes, marker or crayon, heavy smoke or nicotine staining, unauthorized paint colors
Carpet and floorsWorn paths in high-traffic areas, light fading near windows, minor dents from furniturePet urine stains, burns, large tears, deep gouges in hardwood
Windows and blindsSun-faded or slightly dusty blinds, small loose hardwareBroken glass, bent or missing blinds, torn screens
Kitchen and bathWorn grout, minor surface scratches on counters, faded caulkCracked tiles, burns or deep cuts in counters, broken fixtures
AppliancesLoosened handles, age-related failureBroken doors or racks, damage from misuse
CleanlinessLight dust, minor residue from normal livingGrease buildup, mold from neglect, trash or belongings left behind

These are general examples, not rules. The same mark can be either, depending on how long you lived there, how old the item was and what caused it.

Age and depreciation

Items wear out on a timeline. Carpet, paint and appliances have a useful life, and many housing agencies and courts consider an item's age when deciding what a tenant should pay. If a landlord charges you the full replacement cost of carpet that was already old when you moved in, that's worth challenging. A reasonable charge reflects the remaining value lost because of the damage, not the cost of upgrading the apartment.

Cleaning is different

Landlords can usually charge for cleaning only to the extent it's needed to return the unit to the cleanliness it had when you moved in. California's statute says this directly. Leaving the home reasonably clean protects you. Charges for "full professional cleaning" on a unit you left tidy are a common target for dispute.

How to challenge a deduction

  1. Ask for the itemized statement and receipts if you haven't received them.
  2. Compare each charge with your photos from move-in and move-out.
  3. Check the item's age. If it was old at move-in, ask for the depreciation calculation.
  4. Check the amount. Do receipts match the charge? Is the work shown to have been done?
  5. Respond in writing and list each charge you dispute and why. Our demand letter guide shows how.
Photos win disputes

Take wide shots and close-ups of every room on move-in day and again on move-out day. A dated photo of a nail hole or a scuff is better than any argument about it.

What about the "move-in" condition?

If you didn't document the unit's condition when you moved in, you're at a disadvantage, but not out of options. In some states, such as Washington, a landlord who collects a deposit must give a written move-in condition statement, and failing to do so can create liability. If you never got one, mention it. Even a few photos from your first week can help.

Quick answers

Can a landlord charge for repainting?

Usually not for ordinary fading or minor scuffs after a normal tenancy. They may charge for unusual damage, such as large marks, smoke staining or unapproved colors, and often only a prorated share if the paint was already old.

Are nail holes normal wear and tear?

A few small nail holes from hanging pictures are commonly treated as normal wear and tear. Large holes, many holes or anchors that damaged the wall are more likely to be damage. Check your lease too.

Can a landlord keep my deposit for carpet cleaning?

In many states, a landlord can charge for cleaning only to restore the unit to its move-in cleanliness, and some leases require professional carpet cleaning. If your lease doesn't require it and the carpet was left clean, challenge the charge.
