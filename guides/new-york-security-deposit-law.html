New York Security Deposit Law: The 14-Day Rule and What Happens If It's Missed

Since the 2019 reforms, New York landlords have 14 days to return your deposit or send an itemized statement. If they miss it, they lose the right to keep any of it.

Updated November 18, 2026 · 3 min read · General information, not legal advice

Key takeaways

  • A landlord must provide an itemized statement and return the remaining deposit within 14 days after you vacate.
  • A landlord who misses the deadline forfeits any right to retain any portion of the deposit.
  • A willful violation can bring punitive damages of up to twice the deposit.
  • You have the right to request a pre-move-out inspection, which lets you fix problems before they're deducted.

New York's rules are in General Obligations Law section 7-108. Major amendments in 2019 shortened the landlord's deadline and strengthened tenant protections. This guide covers the basics for residential tenants. Rent-stabilized apartments and some building types have additional rules, so check those too.

Deposit cap

A security deposit or advance may not exceed one month's rent, with limited exceptions such as certain seasonal-use units and owner-occupied cooperatives.

The 14-day deadline

Within 14 days after you have vacated, the landlord must provide an itemized statement indicating the basis for any amount retained and return the remaining deposit. The NYC Rent Guidelines Board describes the same rule for non-rent-regulated tenants. Use our calculator to count the days.

Late means forfeiture

If the landlord fails to provide the itemized statement and refund within 14 days, they forfeit any right to retain any portion of the deposit. That is a powerful rule: a landlord who is even a few days late with a valid damage claim can lose it.

Willful violations

A landlord who violates the law is liable for actual damages. If the violation is willful, a court may award punitive damages of up to twice the amount of the deposit.

What a landlord can deduct

The deposit is refundable except for reasonable, itemized costs for:

  • Unpaid rent
  • Damage beyond normal wear and tear
  • Unpaid utilities payable directly to the landlord under the lease
  • Moving and storage of your belongings

Deductions for ordinary wear and tear, or for damage caused by a prior tenant, are not allowed. See wear and tear vs. damage.

Inspections

New York law gives you a right to a pre-move-out inspection. After notice that the tenancy is ending, the landlord must notify you in writing of your right to request an inspection and to be present. If you ask, it must take place no earlier than two weeks and no later than one week before the end of the tenancy, with at least 48 hours' written notice. You then have the opportunity to cure any conditions identified before the tenancy ends. If you give less than two weeks' notice, the landlord isn't required to offer one. You also have the right to request an inspection before you move in, so that existing defects are written down.

Ask for the inspection in writing

Reply to the landlord's notice, or send your own, asking for the pre-move-out inspection. Fix what's on the list, and you may avoid deductions entirely.

Interest and how the deposit is held

Deposits must be kept separate from the landlord's own money. In larger buildings, landlords generally must hold deposits in an interest-bearing account in a New York State bank and pay tenants the interest, less a 1% administrative fee. Check the statute and your lease for how this applies to your building.

Steps for New York tenants

  1. Request the pre-move-out inspection in writing.
  2. Take dated photos and video of everything.
  3. Send your forwarding address in writing on move-out day.
  4. Count 14 days from the day you vacated.
  5. If no itemized statement and refund arrive, send a demand letter citing the forfeiture rule.
  6. If needed, file in small claims. Read our guide.

Quick answers

Does New York's 14-day rule apply to every rental?

The 2019 amendments apply to residential rentals generally, with specific rules for certain building types and for rent-stabilized apartments. Check the statute and ask a tenant organization if your situation is unusual.

Can a New York landlord charge a non-refundable fee?

A deposit or advance cannot exceed one month's rent, and the statute treats the deposit as refundable except for the itemized deductions it allows.

What if my landlord claims damage after day 14?

A landlord who fails to provide the itemized statement and refund within 14 days forfeits any right to retain any portion of the deposit.
