Move-Out Checklist: 12 Things to Do Before You Hand Back the Keys

Most deposit problems are preventable. Spend an hour on these twelve steps and you'll be in a much stronger position if your landlord tries to keep your money.

Updated November 18, 2026 · 3 min read · General information, not legal advice

Key takeaways

  • Check your lease's move-out notice rules early. Some states let a landlord enforce them if the lease makes them prominent.
  • Photograph and film everything, and keep files with dates.
  • Give your forwarding address in writing and save proof.
  • Put your state's return deadline in your calendar the day you leave.

The best time to win a security deposit dispute is before it starts. Use this checklist in the last weeks of your tenancy.

Four to six weeks before

  1. Read your lease's move-out clause. Look for notice requirements, cleaning requirements and rules about carpets, keys and forwarding addresses. In Texas, a lease can make advance notice of move-out a condition of refunding your deposit only if the requirement is underlined or in conspicuous bold print.
  2. Give written notice exactly as the lease says, and keep proof of when and how you sent it.
  3. Ask about a pre-move-out inspection. In California, a landlord must tell you in writing about your right to request an initial inspection, and the inspection can happen no earlier than two weeks before the end of the tenancy. New York gives tenants a similar right. It's your chance to fix problems yourself before they become charges.

The week of the move

  1. Take photos and video of every room. Go wide and close-up: walls, floors, ceilings, windows, closets, inside the oven and fridge, bathroom fixtures and any existing damage. Keep original files with their dates.
  2. Clean the way a landlord would check. Kitchen (oven, fridge, cabinets), bathrooms (tub, toilet, sink), floors, baseboards, windows, light fixtures. Cleaning charges are among the most avoidable deductions.
  3. Handle small repairs your lease allows. Replace burned-out bulbs and missing batteries, and, if your lease permits, fill small nail holes. Don't repaint or make major repairs without permission.
  4. Remove everything. Furniture, trash, food, hangers, personal items and anything you stored. Left-behind belongings can trigger disposal charges.
  5. Settle utilities and final bills. Unpaid utilities that fall to the landlord can lawfully come out of your deposit in many states.

On move-out day

  1. Do a final walk-through. Ask the landlord or manager to inspect with you and confirm the condition in writing, even by text or email. Take a last round of photos after the unit is empty.
  2. Return all keys, fobs and parking passes in a way you can prove. Get a receipt or a message confirming the date and time, or mail them with tracking.
  3. Give your forwarding address in writing. Send it by email or letter and keep a copy. In Texas, a landlord isn't required to return the deposit or provide the itemized list until you give a written forwarding address.

After you leave

  1. Mark the deadline and organize your records. Use our calculator to find your return date and save it in your calendar. Keep your lease, photos, notices and messages in one folder.
Don't skip your last month's rent

It can be tempting to tell the landlord to use your deposit for the last month. In many states that is not allowed, and it can backfire. In Texas, a tenant who withholds last month's rent in bad faith can be liable for three times the rent plus the landlord's attorney's fees.

Quick answers

Should I be present for the final inspection?

Yes, whenever possible. Being there lets you correct misunderstandings and fix small issues on the spot. If you can't, take your own photos and ask for the inspection results in writing.

Do I have to hire professional cleaners?

Only if your lease requires it. Check the wording. Otherwise leave the unit as clean as you found it, and document it with photos.

How long should I keep my records?

Keep them at least until you've received your deposit and any dispute is resolved, and ideally for a year or more. Deadlines to sue vary by state.
