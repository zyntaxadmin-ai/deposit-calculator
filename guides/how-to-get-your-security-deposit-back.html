How to Get Your Security Deposit Back: A Step-by-Step Guide

You gave back the keys weeks ago and the money still hasn't come. Here is exactly what to do, in the order it happens, from move-out day to small claims court.

Updated November 18, 2026 · 5 min read · General information, not legal advice

Key takeaways

  • Deposit disputes are usually won with paperwork: dates, photos and written notices beat memory every time.
  • Your state's law sets the deadline, not your lease. In the data behind our calculator, deadlines run from 14 to 60 days after move-out.
  • Give your forwarding address in writing and keep proof. In some states the landlord's duty depends on it.
  • A landlord who misses the deadline can face real consequences, from losing the right to deduct to paying extra damages.

You handed over the keys, took one last look at the empty apartment and drove away. Then nothing. Two weeks pass, then four. Your deposit, often the biggest chunk of cash you need for your next place, is sitting in your old landlord's account.

The good news is that security deposits are among the most heavily regulated parts of renting. Every state has rules about how long a landlord has to respond, what they can deduct, and what happens if they don't follow the rules. This guide walks you through the process in the order it actually unfolds.

1. Find your state's deadline first

The clock that matters is set by state law, not by your lease. Deadlines vary a lot. A few examples from official statutes:

  • California: 21 calendar days after you vacate.
  • Texas: 30 days after you surrender the premises.
  • Florida: 15 days if the landlord isn't keeping anything, or 30 days to send a notice if they plan to make a claim.
  • New York: 14 days after you vacate.

Some states count business days, some start the clock only after you give a forwarding address, and some treat a missed deadline as the end of the landlord's right to deduct anything. Our free calculator works out the date for your state, and our state guides go deeper on the biggest ones.

Put the deadline in your calendar

Add the date to your phone the day you move out, plus a reminder two days earlier. A missed deadline is only useful to you if you notice it.

2. Before you move out: build your evidence

The strongest deposit claims are built before the keys change hands.

  • Read your lease's move-out section. Some leases require advance written notice. In Texas, for example, a notice requirement tied to refunding the deposit only counts if it is underlined or in conspicuous bold print in the lease.
  • Ask about a pre-move-out inspection. Some states give tenants the right to request one shortly before the lease ends, which lets you fix problems before they become deductions. California and New York both do.
  • Take photos and video of everything. Every room, inside closets and appliances, floors, walls, windows and anything that was already damaged. Timestamped files are best.
  • Clean thoroughly. Cleaning charges are among the most common deductions and the easiest to avoid.

Our move-out checklist covers all of this in detail.

3. On move-out day

Return all keys in a way you can prove: hand them over and get a signed or texted acknowledgment, or mail them with tracking. Do a final walk-through with the landlord or manager if they'll do one, and ask them to confirm the unit's condition in writing, even a short email or text. Take one last set of photos after everything is out.

4. Give your forwarding address in writing

Where should the landlord send your money? If they don't have an address, some states let them pause their obligations. In Texas, a landlord is not required to return the deposit or provide the itemized list until you give a written statement of your forwarding address, although you don't lose your right to the refund just because you forgot. Florida's required deposit disclosure tells tenants to give the landlord their new address so notices can reach them.

The simple rule: send your forwarding address in writing (email, text or letter) on or before move-out day, and keep proof of when you sent it.

5. What the landlord has to send you

If a landlord keeps any part of your deposit, most states require a written, itemized statement of each deduction and its cost, often with receipts or invoices. Typical lawful deductions are unpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, and cleaning needed to return the unit to its move-in condition. In California, for example, repair and cleaning deductions generally need supporting documentation once they total more than $125.

What landlords usually cannot charge for is ordinary wear and tear. We explain the difference in normal wear and tear vs. damage.

6. Review the statement line by line

  1. Was it on time? Compare the postmark or email date with your deadline.
  2. Is every charge explained? Vague lines like "repairs" or "misc." are a red flag.
  3. Do the amounts match receipts? You are generally only charged reasonable, actual costs.
  4. Is any charge for ordinary wear? Faded paint and worn carpet from normal use usually isn't chargeable.
  5. Do your photos disagree? Compare each charge with your move-in and move-out pictures.
Watch the objection window

Some states give you a short window to object. In Florida, you have 15 days after receiving the landlord's notice of intention to impose a claim to object in writing. Respond in writing and keep a copy.

7. If the deadline passes or the numbers look wrong

Send a calm, factual demand letter that states the amount you're owed, the deadline you're relying on and a reasonable date to respond. Many landlords pay at this point because they know what a court could award. Penalties vary widely by state. For example:

  • Texas: a landlord who keeps a deposit in bad faith can owe $100 plus three times the amount wrongfully withheld, plus attorney's fees.
  • New York: a landlord who misses the 14-day deadline forfeits the right to keep any part of the deposit, and a willful violation can bring punitive damages of up to twice the deposit.
  • California: up to twice the deposit in statutory damages for a bad-faith retention, on top of actual damages.
  • Washington: a landlord who misses the deadline owes the full deposit, and a court may award up to twice the deposit for intentional refusal.

8. Small claims court

If a letter doesn't work, small claims court is built for exactly this kind of dispute, with simplified procedures and no need for a lawyer. See our guide to suing for your deposit in small claims court.

Mistakes that cost tenants money

  • Skipping the last month's rent to "use up" the deposit. In many states this is not allowed, and Texas law, for one, can make a tenant who does it in bad faith liable for three times the rent withheld plus attorney's fees.
  • No photos. Without evidence, it is your word against theirs.
  • Verbal-only communication. Put everything in writing, even a short text.
  • Missing a notice or objection window that your state sets.
  • Waiting too long. There are time limits to sue. Don't let a dispute drift for months.

Quick answers

Can a landlord keep my whole deposit?

Only for lawful reasons such as unpaid rent or damage beyond normal wear and tear, and in most states only if they follow the rules on timing and itemization. A landlord who misses the deadline can lose the right to keep any part of it in some states, including New York and Florida.

What if I never gave a forwarding address?

In some states, like Texas, the landlord's duty to return the deposit or send an itemized list doesn't start until you give a written forwarding address, but you don't lose your right to the money. Send the address now, in writing, and keep proof.

Do I need a lawyer to get my deposit back?

Usually not. Most deposit disputes are resolved with a demand letter or in small claims court, where lawyers aren't required. Legal aid groups and tenant organizations can help if you're unsure.
