California Security Deposit Law: Deadlines, Limits and Penalties

California gives landlords 21 days, limits how much they can collect, and requires documentation for many deductions. Here's how the rules work in practice.

Updated November 18, 2026 · 4 min read · General information, not legal advice

Key takeaways

  • A California landlord must send an itemized statement and any refund within 21 calendar days after you vacate.
  • Since July 1, 2024, deposits are capped at one month's rent, with a higher cap for some small landlords.
  • Landlords may deduct for unpaid rent, damage beyond ordinary wear and tear, and cleaning to restore move-in cleanliness, but only reasonable amounts.
  • A bad-faith retention can lead to statutory damages of up to twice the deposit, on top of actual damages.

California's security deposit law is one of the more tenant-protective in the country, and it has changed recently. Here is what tenants should know, based on California Civil Code section 1950.5.

The 21-day deadline

No later than 21 calendar days after you've vacated the unit, the landlord must send you an itemized statement of any deductions and return the remaining deposit. Note that the count starts when you vacate, not when your lease ends, and it uses calendar days, not business days. Use our calculator to see your exact date.

How much can a landlord collect?

Under the rules that became operative on July 1, 2024, a landlord generally cannot demand a security deposit of more than one month's rent, in addition to the first month's rent paid at or before move-in. There are exceptions:

  • Small landlords: a natural person, or an LLC whose members are all natural persons, who owns no more than two residential rental properties with no more than four dwelling units total may collect up to two months' rent.
  • Service members: the small-landlord exception does not apply when the tenant is a service member.
  • Older deposits: the cap does not apply to security collected before July 1, 2024.

The statute also says a lease cannot describe any security as "nonrefundable."

What a landlord can deduct

California law lists the purposes for which a landlord may use your deposit:

  • Unpaid rent
  • Repairing damage to the unit beyond ordinary wear and tear, caused by you, your guests or licensees
  • Cleaning needed to return the unit to the level of cleanliness it had when you moved in
  • If the lease allows, remedying future defaults involving personal property

Landlords may claim only amounts reasonably necessary for those purposes. Read more in our guide to normal wear and tear vs. damage.

Receipts, invoices and photos

When a landlord deducts for repairs or cleaning, the statement generally must be accompanied by documentation such as receipts, invoices or, in some cases, a description of the work and its cost. There is an exception: if repair and cleaning deductions together are $125 or less, the landlord does not have to attach that documentation, although a tenant's timely request for it can still require it.

The law also now requires photographs:

  • Beginning April 1, 2025, a landlord must photograph the unit after the tenant returns it and before any deductible repairs or cleaning, and again after those repairs or cleaning are completed.
  • For tenancies beginning on or after July 1, 2025, the landlord must also take photographs immediately before or at the start of the tenancy.

If you receive deductions with no photos or receipts, ask for them in writing.

The initial inspection

A California landlord must notify you in writing of your option to request an initial inspection, and your right to be present, either within a reasonable time after notice of termination or before the lease ends. If you ask for one, it must happen at a reasonable time, no earlier than two weeks before the end of the tenancy, and generally with at least 48 hours' written notice. This is a valuable chance to fix problems yourself, which can reduce deductions.

Request the inspection in writing

Send a short email asking for the initial inspection and the date range. Then you have proof you asked, and you'll know exactly what the landlord plans to deduct before you leave.

What happens if the landlord is late or acts in bad faith?

If a landlord retains security in bad faith, a court may award statutory damages of up to twice the amount of the security, in addition to actual damages. The statute also provides that a landlord who in bad faith fails to comply with the itemization and timing requirements is not entitled to claim any of the security. Some cities have additional local rules, so check your city's tenant ordinance.

Steps for California tenants

  1. Request the initial inspection in writing before you move out.
  2. Take dated photos and video, and keep them.
  3. Send your forwarding address in writing on move-out day.
  4. Calendar day 21 after you vacate.
  5. If the statement is late, missing receipts or unfair, send a demand letter.
  6. If needed, file in small claims. See our small claims guide.

*Higher cap for some small landlords, as described above.

Quick answers

Can a California landlord keep my deposit for normal wear and tear?

No. The statute allows deductions for damage beyond ordinary wear and tear, not for the ordinary effects of living in the unit.

Does the 21 days start when my lease ends?

No. It starts after you have vacated the premises.

What if my landlord sends the statement on day 21 but it's missing receipts?

Ask in writing for the documentation. Deductions for repairs and cleaning that exceed $125 together generally need supporting documentation, and a landlord's failure to comply in bad faith can cost them the right to claim any of the deposit.
