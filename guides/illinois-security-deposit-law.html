Illinois Security Deposit Law: 30 Days, 45 Days and the 5-Unit Rule

Illinois has a 30-day clock for itemized statements and a 45-day clock for refunds, but the main statute covers only buildings with five or more units. Here's how it works.

Updated November 18, 2026 · 3 min read · General information, not legal advice

Key takeaways

  • The Security Deposit Return Act applies to landlords of residential property containing five or more units.
  • To deduct for damage, the landlord must send an itemized statement within 30 days; otherwise the deposit must be returned in full within 45 days.
  • Deductions must be backed by paid receipts, or estimates followed by receipts within 30 days.
  • Where a court finds the landlord refused or acted in bad faith, the penalty is twice the deposit plus costs and attorney's fees.

Illinois deposits are governed by the Security Deposit Return Act (765 ILCS 710). It has some important limits, so start by checking whether it applies to you.

Who is covered?

Section 1 applies to a lessor of residential real property containing five or more units who has received a security deposit from a lessee. If you rent in a smaller building, the Act's specific deadlines and penalties don't apply by their terms, though other protections, such as your lease, local ordinances and general contract law, may. A legal aid organization can help you sort out your options. Chicago also has its own Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance with additional rules, so Chicago renters should read it too. Separately, larger complexes may owe interest under Illinois's Security Deposit Interest Act (765 ILCS 715), which applies to buildings with 25 or more units.

The 30-day and 45-day clocks

  • 30 days: a landlord may withhold money for property damage only if, within 30 days after you vacate, they give you an itemized statement of the damage with the estimated or actual cost of each item.
  • Receipts: the statement should attach paid receipts. If the landlord gives estimates, the paid receipts must follow within 30 days after the statement.
  • 45 days: if the landlord doesn't furnish a compliant statement and receipts, they must return the security deposit in full within 45 days after you vacated.

How the statement must be delivered

The Act allows delivery in person, by postmarked mail to your last known address, or, under current versions, by email to an address you have verified for that purpose. Deposits are returned to your last known address or another address you provide. If you don't provide a mailing or email address, the landlord may not be liable for resulting damages or penalties, so give your forwarding address in writing.

What if the landlord doesn't comply?

If a circuit court finds that the landlord refused to provide the itemized statement, or provided it in bad faith, and failed to return the deposit within the time limits, the landlord is liable for an amount equal to twice the amount of the security deposit due, together with court costs and reasonable attorney's fees.

Check the current text

The statute has been amended over time and different versions are online. Always confirm the current wording on the Illinois General Assembly website or with a legal aid organization before relying on a deadline.

Steps for Illinois tenants

  1. Find out whether your building has five or more units.
  2. Take dated photos and video when you move out.
  3. Send your forwarding address in writing.
  4. Calendar day 30 and day 45 after you vacate.
  5. If the statement is late or unsupported, send a demand letter.
  6. If needed, file in small claims. See our guide.

Quick answers

I rent in a duplex. Does the Act apply?

Not by its terms, since it covers lessors of buildings with five or more units. Other protections may apply, including your lease and local rules. Ask a legal aid organization.

What if the landlord sends an estimate instead of receipts?

The landlord must provide the paid receipts within 30 days after furnishing the estimated statement.

Does the landlord owe me interest?

Not under the Return Act itself. A separate Illinois law requires interest in larger complexes, and Chicago has its own rules. Check the specifics for your building.
